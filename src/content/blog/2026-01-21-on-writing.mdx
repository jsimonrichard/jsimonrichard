Main things I want to hit:

- You should write because it helps you think
  - It requires you to do your own research and come up with a viewpoint that's truly your own
  - For this reason, you should be writing about what you care about (things that are worth thinking deeply about), not just what you know already and spend time doing (i.e. I shouldn't just write about AI research or programming).
- Writing not only helps you think. It helps you remember (as stated by...)
- In a world filled with AI, you should be writing more, not less
  - As stated above, the point is to clarify/improve your own thinking, not to get clicks.
  - AI improves our ability to research, but it also has the ability to take over our reasoning process
  - For those who have less skill as a writer, AI can a great tool to help you articulate things... but it's important to catch yourself if you start relying on it for epistemic work.
  - AI is also great for "rubber ducking"
- I would love to see a world in which _explicit_ caveats translated to more credibility
  - Our news feeds (no matter your political orientation) are overflown with sensational content. But most of the topics they cover (and most of the topics that people care about) are too complex to be captured in a headline.
  - Writing about complex issues should help you think responsibly about those issues.
  - Often, being honest about complex issues means being okay stating the caveats to your argument, or at least acknowledging other view points with out "straw man"-ing them.

- I'm going to practice what I preach...
  - Caveat #1: Writing (and Publishing) what you think makes it harder to change your mind
    - In public, someone who changes their mind often might be seen as unreliable or insincere. And this might be true in some cases, especially when politicians change their "personal" position to gain votes.
    - But, in a world where most "stick to their guns", someone changing their mind should signal that
      - they're humble---i.e. willing to admit they're wrong
      - they're improving---i.e. spending time to think carefully about what they believe.
    - That said, it's not always best to publish what you write. Depending on the topic, it may be better to keep it private until you are more confident about your argument. Or it may be best to never publish it at all. Remember, the point is not to get clicks, it's to improve your thinking.
    - If you do want to publish, you might consider setting expectations for your readers:
      > This article is not optimized for virality or completeness. It's a snapshot of my thought process on X...

  - Caveat #2: AI can quickly become a reasoning crutch
    - AI is great at widening your research "surface area", but it can also narrow your thinking by always responding positively to the ideas you posit. Remember, AI doesn't have beliefs (footnote on alignment). It's just been trained to produce coherent, conversational answers.
    - This is a feature, not a bug. Can you imagine how 1) damaging and 2) unappealing an AI model would be if its creators gave it beliefs by fiat?
      - Damaging because some would be naive enough to believe the model without checking it's work
      - Unappealing because those who disagree would a) not trust the model on anything else it says and b) rarely be in the mood to listen to something they strongly disagree with.
      - I realize there's some contradiction between these two items on the grounds of "trust" versus "checking their work". But it's impossible to verify everything that a model produces, and in general we hope that AI models can be trusted to provide good answers on the basis of "common knowledge".
    - As you use AI, remember to treat it as a research tool rather than the final arbiter of the question at hand.
    - To defend against this issue when using AI, consider explicitly asking AI for counter arguments, caveats, and the like. And when trying to find that AI's "true opinion" (which is still a helpful/interesting question to ask), give it alternatives. Don't just explain your own argument and then ask for its thoughts.

  - Caveat #3: Explicit caveats may reduce persuasion (and that's okay)
    - Including explicit caveats may reduce the immediate persuasive power, appeal, and virality of your work. But it's my hope that as we become better thinkers, we treat caveats as a signal for trust and long-term credibility.
    - This is the tradeoff that I'm asking you to make.

Our world is increasingly saturated by AI-generated content.
Social media is filled with AI-generated images and videos, software is often generated using AI coding platforms, and every Google search is greeted by an AI summary.
Each of these realities will have a measurable impact on society, but I am increasingly concerned about the impact that AI-generated text is having on argumentative writing and critical thinking.
A friend of mine, who is a TA at Purdue, recently lamented that one of her students supported an answer with the phrase "[ChatGPT] is god."
While it usually isn't this explicit, most TAs, professors, and school teachers deal regularly with students who want AI think for them.
This isn't new---students have always looked for ways to avoid work---but it is so much easier now.

Separately (although not as separately as I'd like), we live in a world of constant outrage.
Neither side of the political spectrum can agree on the facts, and every few weeks one side accuses the other of something "unthinkable."
Much of this lives at the national level, but it also infects the conversations of friends, family, and the local community.
_A_ primary cause of this outrage and division is, I believe, that many who participate in public discourse try avoid the slow, painful processes of critical thinking.

As I was reflecting on these issues, I realized that I want to improve my own habits.
Although I consume a fair amount of news and commentary, I don't spend enough time researching, weighing alternative viewpoints, or carefully articulating the opinions I've formed.
It's true that life is busy, but I believe that thinking carefully through public issues is a matter of "loving your neighbor" ([Matthew 22:37-40](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2022%3A37-40&version=NIV)), something that all Christians are called to do. In the remainder of this post, I will outline why writing is (still) the best way to focus such thinking.
